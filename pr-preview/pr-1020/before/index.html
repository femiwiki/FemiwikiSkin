    This page exists to be looked at. It is one page carrying every element a skin has to style, so that a skin author can bake it and see the whole surface at once instead of discovering, three sites later, that nobody ever gave the definition lists a margin. Nothing here is documentation; if a sentence reads like filler, it is filler, and it is here because running text is the only way to judge a measure and a line height.

    Bake this directory with your own skin listed in .wikven.yaml. It sets WikvenBuildFor: skin-preview, so wikven leaves the chrome alone and what you see is your skin's own work: its personal menu, its tabs, its toolbox. See Second page for what a second page and a sidebar entry look like, and Second page/A subpage with a rather long title for a subpage.

    Headings

    [edit]

    The heading above is a level two, which is the first one a page body can hold: the level one is the page title, drawn by the skin rather than by the wikitext. What follows is the rest of the ladder, which is worth seeing in one column because the gap between a level four and a level five is where a type scale usually gives up.

    Level three

    [edit]

    Sits under a level two, and most skins still give it a rule or a weight of its own.

    Level four

    [edit]

    By here a skin has usually run out of size and started using weight or colour instead.

    Level five
    [edit]

    Rare in real pages, common in the templates that generate them.

    Level six
    [edit]

    The last one MediaWiki has. If it is indistinguishable from bold body text, that is a decision rather than an accident, but it should be a decision.

    Running text

    [edit]

    A paragraph of ordinary prose, long enough to wrap several times at any sensible measure, because a line height that looks generous on two lines can look loose on eight. The words are chosen to be unremarkable. Their job is to be a grey block at arm's length and a readable one up close, and a skin that gets the first right and the second wrong is a skin that was only ever looked at in a screenshot.

    A second paragraph, to show what a skin does with the space between two of them. Some skins separate paragraphs with a margin and some with an indent, and the two look very different once there are five in a row. This one also runs long enough to wrap, since the interesting question is not what one line does but what the block does.

    A third, shorter one, because not every paragraph is a wall.

    Inline formatting

    [edit]

    Text can be bold, italic, both at once, struck through, small, monospaced, or carry a superscript and a subscript. It can hold an internal link, a red link to a page that does not exist, an external link, and an external link with no visible protocol. A skin that colours these four identically has thrown away information the reader uses.

    It can also hold a token no line break will fit: ThisIsAnUnreasonablyLongIdentifierNobodyWouldWriteButSomebodyAlwaysDoes. That is here because an unbreakable run is what puts a horizontal scrollbar on a phone, and the only way to know whether your skin wraps it is to look at it.

    And here is the paragraph that has to flow around it. It needs to be long enough to actually reach past the bottom of the float, because the interesting failure is not the text beside the box but the heading that comes after it and forgets to clear. Skins that set a clearfix on headings look fine here; skins that do not will show it in the next section, which is exactly why this paragraph goes on for as long as it does rather than stopping at a polite two lines.

    Images

    [edit]

    A thumbnail is the most-styled image element on a wiki: it has a frame, a caption, a float, and a magnify affordance, and each of them is somebody's stylesheet. The paragraph beside it is here so the float has something to float against, and it runs on for a few lines so that the wrap is visible rather than theoretical.

    Left floats are rarer and therefore less tested. The text beside this one exists for the same reason as the text beside the last one, and if the two paragraphs read alike that is because their job is the same.

    Preformatted and code

    [edit]

and does not wrap, so it is the other way a page ends up with a horizontal scrollbar.

    A pre block, which is the same element by a different route.

    The rule above is a horizontal rule, which is the last block element MediaWiki draws and the one most often left at the browser default.
